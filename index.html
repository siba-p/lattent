Microsoft’s MatterGen and Google DeepMind’s GNoME showed a new way to find materials: generate candidates, screen them with machine-learned property models, and check them against computed data. Lattent brings that loop to your bench at the level of chemical composition, next to the 5.8 million DFT structures of the open Alexandria database.

Lattent’s own masked-diffusion model, trained on the Materials Project, proposes formulas in your chemical system, steered towards a band gap. Property networks predict formation energy (MAE 0.09 eV/atom) and band gap, and the database shows which candidates are new.

Spectra, diffraction and mechanical tests

Lattent understands the measurements materials labs run every day. Fit overlapping XRD, Raman or XPS peaks on a fitted baseline; get d-spacings, Scherrer crystallite size and Williamson–Hall strain; extract optical band gaps from UV–Vis; and read modulus, yield, strength and toughness from stress–strain curves.

  • Gaussian, Lorentzian and pseudo-Voigt multi-peak fitting
  • Tauc analysis for direct and indirect transitions
  • Area under the curve, Savitzky–Golay smoothing and derivatives

Fit any model, then let the data choose

Thirty-five equations from materials science, polymers, nanomaterials and chemistry (Arrhenius, Langmuir and Sips, Avrami, Carreau, WLF, Hall–Petch, Tafel and more), or type your own. Lattent's shape model recognises what kind of behaviour your data show and narrows the equations to the plausible ones. Auto-fit then ranks them by AICc, so you see what the data support.

  • Confidence intervals and confidence bands, derived quantities such as Eₐ in kJ/mol and eV
  • Constraints, weighting, residual plots and runs test
  • Plain-language warnings when a parameter is poorly determined

From your data to the next experiment

Lattent learns a Gaussian-process model of a property from the experiments you have run, such as the toughness of a polymer blend, the yield of a synthesis or the conductivity of an electrolyte. It shows which inputs matter and proposes the next experiments most likely to improve the result, respecting mixture constraints. Add them to your table, run them, and repeat.

  • Bayesian optimisation with expected improvement, in batches
  • Cross-validated model accuracy, so you know when to trust it
  • PCA, k-means clustering and correlation for exploring datasets

Statistics with the checks built in

t tests, Mann–Whitney, one-way ANOVA, Welch's ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis, with Tukey and Holm–Šidák post tests. Lattent checks equal variance and normality for you, suggests the right test when an assumption fails, and draws significance stars on the graph.

  • Descriptive statistics, normality tests, Grubbs outliers
  • Effect sizes and confidence intervals, not only p-values
  • Correlation matrices and multiple linear regression

An AI lab partner that reads, checks and never makes up a number

Open Facet, Lattent’s AI lab partner, on any table. Its built-in suggestions come from Lattent's own shape model, a neural network trained on 67,600 simulated lab datasets across 13 families of behaviour. It recognises a spectrum, an absorption edge, a thermally activated trend or a saturating isotherm, and proposes the right analysis with one-click Run. Facet also checks your whole project for errors (missing units, too few points, poorly determined parameters, the wrong test), reads graphs (drop a picture of a plot and it traces the data into a table) and runs as an autonomous report agent: it plans the analyses, runs them, checks them and writes the report. Connect your own Claude or GPT key to ask in plain language or attach a figure to have it interpreted and critiqued; the language model plans and explains, and Lattent's validated engine does the maths.

Questions

How is this different from Origin or GraphPad Prism?

Lattent is built around the measurements materials and chemistry labs actually make (XRD, spectra, band gaps, isotherms, Arrhenius data, tensile tests), with curve fitting, statistics and publication graphs underneath, plus a shape model, Bayesian optimisation and Facet, an AI lab partner. It runs in any browser with nothing to install and costs a fraction of a desktop licence. It does not yet do everything those programs do, such as 3D plots or two-way ANOVA; those are on the roadmap.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

Not unless you choose to. Calculations run in your browser and projects are kept in your browser. If you save a project to the cloud, it is stored in a database where row-level security lets only you, and your lab if you share it, read it. The optional AI chat sends data directly from your browser to the provider you chose, with your own key. Details on the security page.

Is payment secure?

Yes. Checkout is run by Lemon Squeezy, a PCI-compliant merchant of record that also handles tax. Lattent never sees your card. Your plan is activated by a signed message from Lemon Squeezy to our server, never by your browser.

How do I know the numbers are right?

Every analysis is checked against SciPy and NumPy, the reference scientific Python libraries, on test datasets before each release: parameters, standard errors, p-values, Tukey intervals and Gaussian-process predictions. The fitter is also benchmarked on NIST’s certified nonlinear-regression problems, where it gets 51 of 52 fits right to at least four significant digits. See the validation page.

I have never used curve-fitting software. Where do I start?

Open a worked example close to your field, look at what Lattent reports, then replace the numbers with yours. Or create a table, paste your data and press Suggest an analysis.

Do I need an AI subscription?

No. The built-in suggestions need no key. The chat is optional and uses your own Anthropic or OpenAI key, billed by them at their API rates, usually cents per conversation.

Can my lab pay with a purchase order or grant?

Yes. Lemon Squeezy issues a proper invoice with your institution's details; for purchase orders, use the Contact link at the foot of this page.

Does Lattent really generate new materials?

It proposes new chemical compositions that its models predict to be stable and close to a target band gap, and tells you which are absent from a database of 5.8 million computed structures. That is a shortlist for a DFT calculation or a synthesis, not a discovery in itself: the generator works at the level of composition, not crystal structure, and composition-only predictions have errors of about 0.09 eV/atom for formation energy and 0.3 eV for the band gap. The methods and test results are on the validation page.

Is it suitable for regulated or clinical work?

No. Lattent is research software and is not validated for GLP, 21 CFR Part 11 or diagnostic use.
